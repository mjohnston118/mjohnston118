# General areas of research

Trained as a cognitive development researcher, my interests span a wide range of phenomena. My research sits at the intersection of cognitive, social, and emotional development, with a particular focus on how children and adults learn from experience, anticipate the future, and regulate their behaviour accordingly. Below is an overview of my main research projects, spanning my PhD, postdoctoral work, and independently funded studies.

# Current and previous projects

<details markdown="1">
<summary markdown="span">Development of Spontaneous Episodic Future Thinking and Links to the Home Environment</summary>

This project combines expertise from my PhD and postdoc on temporal cognition and cognitive control to explore the development of episodic future thinking (EFT) — the ability to project oneself into the future to vividly pre-experience events. EFT is critical for planning and healthy decision-making (Schacter et al., 2017); envisioning future outcomes can reduce impulsive behaviours, as shown in studies with substance users and smokers (Snider et al., 2016; Stein et al., 2016).

The project has two strands. First, improving how EFT is assessed in children — there is ongoing debate about how best to operationalise it (Martin-Ordas, 2020), and I have developed a task measuring spontaneous future thinking use. Second, exploring how the immediate environment shapes EFT. Little research has examined how factors like socioeconomic status (SES) relate to children's future-oriented thinking; delay discounting studies suggest lower-SES children may prefer smaller-immediate rewards (Tunney, 2020), but it's unclear whether this reflects genuine difficulty imagining the future, or a more adaptive strategy given their circumstances. My work directly assesses EFT and its links to SES and other environmental factors.

I am currently writing up pilot data showing a negative association between future-oriented cognition and household organisation:

- Johnston, M., Dolan, J., Chevalier, N. (in prep). Examining links between parent-reported household organisation and children's future thinking and proactive control processes

<summary markdown="span">Developmental Dynamics Between Statistical Learning and Cognitive Control</summary>

As lead researcher on this project at the University of Edinburgh, I investigated how statistical learning and cognitive control develop and interact across childhood. This involved a longitudinal study (N=187) combining behavioural, eye-tracking, and EEG data, including a novel statistical learning task I developed and programmed. Across two papers (both published at Child Development), I demonstrated that SL is multifaceted and that younger children have an enhanced ability to retain and re-apply (some types of) previously learned structures to novel scenarios.

- Johnston, M., Dolan, J., Farkas, B., Chevalier, N. (accepted at Child Development, July 2026, indicative publication August 2026). Age-Related Changes in How Previous Learning Experiences Impact Subsequent Visual Statistical Learning and Relate to Executive Function: A Longitudinal Study. Child Development. [Preprint](https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/xj5e2)
- Johnston, M., Dolan, J., Farkas, B., Chevalier, N. (2026). *Statistical learning in childhood: dimensions, developmental trajectory, and relation with cognitive control.* Child Development. [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1093/chidev/aacag031)
- Smita, R., Johnston, M., Dolan, J., Chevalier, N. (2025). *Post-error slowing and error processing in middle childhood: Evidence for increasing cognitive control adjustment with age.* [Preprint](https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/yzrpf)
- Johnston, M., Dolan, J., Chevalier, N. (in prep). Cognitive demands impact statistical learning in both adults and children.

<summary markdown="span">Compensatory Mechanisms in Older Adults</summary>

This line of research explores compensatory mechanisms in cognitive health and ageing, an area of growing importance given the population over 60 is projected to double by 2050 (WHO, 2020). During my postdoc, I obtained a **Carnegie Trust** grant (£12,612) and tested whether declining cognitive control in later life enhances SL skills, as has been proposed in young children (Saffran, 2020). This work with Kelly Wolfe (Heriot-Watt) is under review at Psychology & Aging.

- Johnston, M., Wolfe, K. (under review at Psychology & Ageing). Statistical learning abilities are negatively associated with cognitive control in older adults.
- See the [Outreach page](/mjohnston118/outreach/) for presentations on this project at the Healthy Ageing Showcase (Heriot-Watt University) and the Gorilla Experiment Builder "Meet the Scientist" webinar.

Continuing this collaboration, we are currently testing the feasibility of an online collaborative-learning task, which older adults can complete independently from their homes (important for testing vulnerable populations that struggle to travel to labs). Due to declines in cognitive control, reliance on collaborative learning and the ability to work with other people increases in later life. See the [Outreach page](/mjohnston118/outreach/) for a video of this task designed in Gorilla Experiment Builder.

<summary markdown="span">The Development and Function of Relief</summary>

My PhD and related collaborative work explored how the emotion of relief develops in childhood, and how anticipating future emotions can shape real-world decisions.

#### The Development of Relief

My PhD thesis examined children's understanding of relief, including both counterfactual relief (comparing what happened to what could have happened) and temporal relief (comparing a bad state to its ending). This involved five experiments (N=539) with children aged 4–12. I also worked within a multi-disciplinary team to explore relief in everyday life in adults.

Across these studies, we demonstrate that in adulthood there are clear psychological distinctions between counterfactual and temporal relief. Given that counterfactual relief was associated with greater counterfactual reasoning — a complex ability to imagine alternative events — it would emerge later in childhood than temporal relief. However, when exploring the development of the ability to attribute these emotions to others, we found little evidence that they emerge at different ages in childhood.

- Feeney, A., Lorimer, S., Graham, A. J., Hoerl, C., Beck, S. R., Johnston, M., & McCormack, T. (2025) Counterfactual Relief. Memory & Cognition.
- Johnston, M., McCormack, T., Lorimer, S., Corbett, B., Beck, S.R., Hoerl, C., Feeney, A. (2024). *Relieved or disappointed? Children's understanding of how others feel at the cessation of events.* Journal of Experimental Child Psychology. [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jecp.2024.106016)
- Graham, A.J., McCormack, T., Lorimer, S., Hoerl, C., Beck, S.R., Johnston, M., Feeney, A. (2023). *Relief in everyday life.* Emotion. [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1037/emo0001193)
- Johnston, M. (2022). *The Development of Relief.* PhD Thesis, Queen's University Belfast. [Link](https://pure.qub.ac.uk/en/studentTheses/the-development-of-relief)
- Johnston, M., McCormack, T., Graham, A.J., Lorimer, S., Beck, S.R., Hoerl, C., Feeney, A. (2022). *Children's understanding of counterfactual and temporal relief in others.* Journal of Experimental Child Psychology. [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jecp.2022.105491)
- Lorimer, S., McCormack, T., Jaroslawska, A.J., Hoerl, C., Beck, S.R., Johnston, M., Feeney, A. (2022). *From Brexit to Biden: What responses to national outcomes tell us about the nature of relief.* Social Psychological and Personality Science. [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1177/19485506211066712)

#### How Anticipating Future Emotions Can Promote Healthy Decision-Making

Working with a multidisciplinary team, I helped design studies exploring how anticipating regret and relief can shift people's intentions toward healthy decisions — including vaccination uptake and cancer screening — using control groups and pre/post-testing to establish causality.

- Lorimer, S., McCormack, T., Hoerl, C., Johnston, M., Beck, S.R., Feeney, A. (2025). *An experimental investigation of the effects of anticipating regret and relief on intentions and decisions to get the influenza vaccination.* Social Science & Medicine. [Link](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0277953625008482)
- Lorimer, S., McCormack, T., Hoerl, C., Beck, S.R., Johnston, M., Feeney, A. (2025). *Testicular self-examination: The role of anticipated relief and anticipated regret.* British Journal of Health Psychology. [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1111/bjhp.12756)
- Lorimer, S., McCormack, T., Hoerl, C., Johnston, M., Beck, S.R., Feeney, A. (2024). *Do both anticipated relief and anticipated regret predict decisions about influenza vaccination?* British Journal of Health Psychology. [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1111/bjhp.12711)

As part of this line of research I co-designed and programmed a risk-taking task to explore how the emotions of regret and relief impact children and adults in their decisions. This is data is currently being analysed and written up for publication.
